Neurodata Governance Toolkit Explorer

Decision support for cross-border neurodata sharing across the data-to-model lifecycle: the four tools, the workflow, and ten worked examples. Version 1.0, October 2026.

Why a toolkit

Neuroscience depends on sharing, analysing and reusing data about the structure and activity of the human nervous system across institutions and borders. The technical side of that exchange is largely solved by community standards and repositories. The governance side is not: the legal conditions attached to a dataset rarely survive transfer, deposit, linkage or transformation into features, embeddings and trained models. A comparative analysis of 93 jurisdictions in Africa, Europe, Latin America and the Caribbean, plus ten governance units in the United States and Canada, found that neurodata are almost never a distinct legal object, that cross-border sharing is permitted in principle but conditional in practice, and that protection depends on the coordinated operation of legal, institutional, technical and contractual safeguards rather than on statute alone.

The toolkit turns those findings into a sequence of four scientist-facing instruments. Tool 1 classifies the specific object by its functional exposure, what it can reveal or enable regardless of its technical form. Tool 2 asks whether a proposed transfer or access pathway can carry the safeguards that tier requires. Tool 3 reassesses the object when it is transformed into derivatives or models. Tool 4 maps the results to the modules of a Modular Neurodata Transfer and Access Agreement (MDTAA). This site presents the tools, the workflow that connects them and ten worked examples.

Three ideas the tools are built on

Functional exposure is the capacity of a neurodata object or derivative to support identification, inference, reconstruction, profiling or prediction about a person or group. Continuity of protection is the preservation of governance obligations as data move across jurisdictions, repositories and computational forms. Conditional interoperability is the finding that legal permission to share is necessary but never sufficient; the pathway has to be made navigable by aligned safeguards.

How the tools fit together

Tool 1 classifies the object for a specific recipient; Tool 2 looks up the origin→destination compatibility class, checks origin-side restrictions (export prohibitions, localisation, regulator authorisation, sanctions, research permits) and the exporter’s and importer’s legal bases, and decides whether the pathway can carry the tier’s safeguards; Tool 3 reassesses derivatives and models, with a documented anonymity test as the gate for public release; Tool 4 maps everything to the modules of the MDTAA, including the transfer-instrument module for the origin regime. Linkage, AI use and any material change loop back to the earlier tools.

Tier 1 — Anonymised aggregate outputs
Group-level or summary information with no participant-level record and no reasonable small-group disclosure risk. Minimal risk of re-identification or individual inference.
  • Group-level activation maps (voxel-wise averages)
  • Aggregated connectivity matrices
  • Summary statistics (e.g., mean amplitudes by group)
  • Published group results with no individual-level data
1b
Tier 1b — Participant-level, non-linkable neurodata
Participant-level records with identifiers, identifying anatomy and metadata removed, no linkage key available to the intended recipient, and a documented disclosure-risk assessment for that recipient (for public release: any member of the public). Recipient-relative.
  • Defaced structural MRI released without any key
  • EEG/MEG with identifying metadata removed and key destroyed
  • Participant-level summary derivatives that reveal no more than the source
  • Synthetic participant-level data with reproduction excluded by testing
2
Tier 2 — Pseudonymised / controlled-access neurodata
Participant-level data linkable to an individual through a code, repository record or institutional key available to the recipient; re-identification risk mitigated but not eliminated.
  • Pseudonymised EEG or fMRI time series
  • Structural MRI (T1-weighted) with participant code
  • Clinical neuropsychological scores with codes
  • Data stored in controlled-access repositories
3
Tier 3 — Directly identifiable or intrinsically traceable neurodata
Data containing direct identifiers, recognisable anatomy, identifying acquisition metadata or intrinsic signal characteristics that permit identification or tracing without reliance on a separately protected key.
  • Non-defaced structural MRI; raw DICOM with headers
  • EEG/MEG/ECoG with identifying metadata or demonstrated fingerprinting capacity
  • Implant telemetry or BCI logs with device identifiers
  • Neurodata linked to names, voice or video
4
Tier 4 — Functionally revealing derivative neurodata
Transformed data with excess inferential capacity: inference outside the authorised purpose, re-identification by the derivative alone, or reconstruction of stimuli, speech, imagery or signals. Standard summary derivatives retain the source tier.
  • Connectome fingerprints and participant-unique embeddings
  • Decoders reconstructing stimuli, speech or imagery
  • Cognitive, emotional or behavioural profiles beyond the approved purpose
  • Risk scores and biomarkers used for prohibited inferences
5
Tier 5 — Model-associated or computationally persistent neurodata
Neural information dispersed across AI models, synthetic-data generators or computational infrastructure with continuing functional exposure, where inference, extraction or reconstruction may be possible without the original dataset.
  • Model weights trained on neurodata
  • Latent representations in generative models
  • Federated-learning updates and model gradients
  • Synthetic neurodata generators; persistent embedding stores
Key principle Tier assignment is informed by functional exposure; legal status remains determined under applicable law. The highest applicable tier governs; an Unknown answer takes the more protective tier; participant status (Step 0B) can raise the minimum tier.

The tools give governance decision support and structured records. They do not establish that a transfer is lawful and do not replace ethics, repository, institutional or legal review.
